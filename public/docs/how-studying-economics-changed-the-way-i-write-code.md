# How studying economics changed the way I write code

I started writing backend code in the second year of my finance and financial-technology bachelor's. I assumed, at the time, that the two halves of my life were going to live in separate rooms. The degree would be the thing I had to finish, and the code would be the thing I actually cared about. I imagined I would eventually stop attending economics lectures the way most engineers stop attending their humanities electives — politely, gratefully, and forever.

Three or four years in, almost all of it spent inside core banking systems, I now think the opposite was true. The degree quietly turned out to be the most useful part of my engineering instinct. It did not teach me about pointers or scheduling or distributed transactions. It taught me a way of seeing systems that I would have had to absorb the hard way otherwise, by breaking things in production until the patterns sank in. This post is my attempt to name a few of those things, because I keep noticing them in code reviews and design discussions, and I think other developers might find them useful even without taking the long way around through coursework.

## Data is a story, not a state

The first habit my degree gave me is the habit of treating data as a record of events rather than a snapshot of state. In accounting, you are taught from the first week that a balance is never a fact you store. It is a derived thing, the sum of a series of recorded changes. The notebook does not say "Alice has one hundred." It says "on Tuesday we wrote down fifty, on Wednesday we wrote down another fifty." If you ever lose the notebook, you have lost everything. If the notebook disagrees with the balance, you trust the notebook.

When I started writing backend code, I noticed that most tutorials default to the opposite shape. They teach you to keep a row, and to update the row in place when something changes. The row is the truth. The history, if you keep any, is logging. That works for a lot of things. It does not work for anything where a regulator, an auditor, or a furious customer might one day ask you to justify a number, because by then the row is whatever you most recently set it to, and the reasons are gone.

The way I write services now, I keep finding myself reaching first for the event-shaped design and only collapsing to a mutable row when I am sure nobody will ever ask me to explain how we got there. Even outside of finance, this turns out to be the right default more often than I expected. Order systems, audit logs, feature-flag history, anything with a workflow — they all benefit from the same shape, and the shape is the one accounting taught me before I had ever written a line of Go.

## The books must balance

The second habit is harder to name. It is something like an accountant's reflex about state, the one that says: do not proceed if the starting state is wrong, ever, full stop.

When I designed the day-close workflow in this system, the very first thing I made it do was refuse to start if there were any unresolved transactions hanging around. Not log a warning. Not fix them quietly. Refuse, generate a report listing exactly what was wrong, and wait for a human to come fix it and signal a retry. A more engineering-trained version of me — say, a version that had only ever shipped CRUD apps — would probably have made the workflow more forgiving. Skip the bad ones. Continue. Reconcile later. Move fast.

I think that instinct comes from being graded, as a student, on whether your trial balance balanced. If you handed in a worksheet where the two sides did not match, you did not get partial credit for "the rest of it was reasonable." You got zero, and rightly so, because in accounting a balance that is off by a cent is not a small mistake — it is evidence that you have lost track of something, and you do not know which side has the missing piece. Once you have internalized that, the idea of "let's just continue and clean up the leftovers later" feels physically wrong. It feels the way it feels to be asked to release a build without running the tests. You do not do it. There is no version of you that would do it.

The same instinct shows up in atomicity. If a banking day commits, it commits all the way or not at all. There is no permitted intermediate state in which half the day has been recorded. I notice that I extend this reflex into corners of code that have nothing to do with money. If a request results in three database writes, I want them in one transaction, even when the writes look unrelated, because I have been trained to be suspicious of any partial-success state where the system might end up sitting between two consistent positions.

## Naming things the way the business names them

A surprisingly large part of what my degree gave me is vocabulary. The first time I read the account-type enum in this codebase and saw `active`, `passive`, `counter-active`, `counter-passive`, I did not have to look anything up. Those were the words on my exam papers. An engineer without that background might have rewritten them as `asset`, `liability`, `contra_asset`, `contra_liability`, which is not wrong, but it loses the connection to the actual accountants in the building who will eventually have to argue with the code about edge cases. Speaking their language saves an enormous amount of back-and-forth.

More generally, the degree trained me to write models in the domain's words, not in generic technical ones. A loan in our system is not a "record," it is a contract. Money owed is not a "balance," it is a principal or an interest accrual or a reserve, and each of those has its own account, with its own rules, because the bank's chart of accounts says so. I find that engineers without a finance background often produce schemas that are technically correct but linguistically translated, and the translation creates friction every time a non-engineer needs to confirm whether the system actually does what they want it to do. The closer you can keep the code to the words the business already uses, the cheaper every future conversation becomes.

## Reading specs without flinching

Anyone who has ever worked with a national regulator's reporting format knows that those formats are not designed for the comfort of the developers who have to implement them. The field names are cryptic, the validation rules are arbitrary, the documentation is a PDF translated through a language pipeline you would rather not think about, and the structure was very obviously written by someone who has never had to parse it themselves.

I think my degree made me more patient with those documents than I would otherwise have been. We read a lot of standards in coursework — accounting standards, banking regulations, capital-adequacy frameworks — and the experience of reading those documents teaches you, slowly, that the bureaucratic ugliness is not a bug to be argued with. It is the surface of a system that is trying to be precise about things that resist precision. The cryptic field names are cryptic because the author needed to disambiguate them from forty similar fields in adjacent forms. The arbitrary-seeming rules are arbitrary because they encode a compromise between three different bureaus that had a meeting in 2009.

A developer who has not been through any of that tends to look at a regulator's spec and try to negotiate with it — to write a wrapper that makes the names nicer, or to skip the fields that look unimportant, or to push back on the validation that seems pointless. A developer who has read economics papers as an undergrad is more inclined to just implement the spec faithfully first, and put the niceness in a translation layer afterwards. I have ended up writing a lot of those translation layers, and they are some of the most boring code I have ever produced, and they are also some of the code I am proudest of, because they let everyone else in the system pretend the regulator's format does not exist.

## Risk is a first-class concern, not an afterthought

Finance teaches you to think about risk before you think about reward. You learn to ask "what is the worst case here, and how much of it can I survive?" before you ask "what is the upside?". For most of the people I went to school with, that habit eventually became a way of thinking about investments. For me, it became a way of thinking about code reviews.

When I review a pull request now, the first question I tend to ask is not "is this correct?" — most code is correct in the common case, or it would not have been written. The question I find more useful is "what is the blast radius if this is wrong?". A bug in a status-page widget has a blast radius of approximately zero. A bug in the day-close workflow has a blast radius that includes the regulator, the customers, the auditors, and somebody's job. The amount of caution that ought to go into reviewing each of those is wildly different, and the size of the PR is not a reliable signal of which one you are looking at.

I notice that engineers without finance training sometimes review every PR with the same intensity, which is exhausting and slow, or with intensity calibrated to the size of the diff, which is wrong. Finance training calibrates you to the consequences, which is the only calibration that actually matters.

## Boring is a feature

The last and probably most important thing my degree gave me is a deep appreciation for boring code. Banking, more than any other domain I have worked in, punishes cleverness. The clever solution is the one that does something unexpected when the inputs are unusual, and the inputs are always eventually unusual. The boring solution is the one that any other engineer can read at three in the morning and immediately know what it does and what it does not do.

When I am writing infrastructure code now, I have to actively resist the urge to be clever, because most of my favorite tricks were learned from engineers who do not have to live with the long tail of consequences that a banking system has. A clever way to compute account balances is a way that will, sooner or later, produce a balance that nobody can explain. A boring way to compute account balances is a way that you can step through with a pencil if you have to, and pencils do not lie.

I think the cultural difference between engineering and finance is that engineering rewards making hard things look easy, and finance rewards making hard things look exactly as hard as they are. The bank does not want a clever loan calculator. It wants a loan calculator whose output matches, line for line, the spreadsheet that the accountant on the third floor has been maintaining for fifteen years. Anything fancier is a liability.

## What I would tell second-year me

If I could go back to the version of me who was about to start writing backend code in the second year of a finance degree, I would say: you are not betraying your degree by switching tracks. You are stacking the most useful possible combination of skills, even if it does not look that way yet. The economics will not stop being relevant. It will keep being relevant in ways that are quieter than the lectures suggest — in the way you reach for event-shaped models without thinking, in the way you refuse to keep going on a broken starting state, in the way you read a regulator's PDF without trying to argue with it, in the way you keep your code boring on purpose.

The engineers I work with who never studied any of this are not worse engineers than I am. They are excellent. But they have had to learn, slowly and from incidents, the things that I happened to be handed early and on purpose. If you are a developer thinking about whether to take a finance class, or to pick up an accounting textbook for fun, or to spend a quarter reading the actual regulatory documents your company is supposed to comply with — yes, do it. It pays back forever. It pays back in places you would not have predicted, and it makes you better at the part of the job that most of the tutorials cannot teach you, which is the part where you have to design something whose mistakes are going to be expensive.

I did not expect any of this when I started. I thought I was going to be an engineer who happened to have a finance degree. It turned out I am a finance person who happens to write code, and the code is better for it.
